Teardown № 01 · scroll-driven animation

How a scroll position
becomes a frame.

Everything scroll-driven on the web — pinned sections, parallax, scrubbed video, the lot — is the same six steps. Here they are, one at a time, with a figure that redraws itself as you read.

~9 minvanilla js, no libraries source ↗

Step 01

The browser gives you one number.

window.scrollY is how far the document has moved past the top of the viewport, in pixels. That's it. It knows nothing about your sections, your animation, or how far through anything the reader is.

It also updates faster than you can paint. Every scroll handler you write is already behind.

Step 02

Normalise it against a range.

Pick a start and an end — usually where a section enters and leaves — and turn the raw pixel count into 0 → 1. Every later decision reads that one number instead of the scroll position, which is what makes the effect independent of page length, viewport size and zoom.

Clamp it. An unclamped progress value is how elements end up half a screen off on short viewports.

Step 03

Pin the stage.

Progress needs somewhere to happen. position: sticky holds an element still while its taller parent scrolls past — so the reader keeps scrolling, but the thing they're looking at stays put. The parent's height is the animation's duration, expressed in pixels.

This is the whole of what GSAP's pin does. It just also handles the edge cases you'd hit on day three.

Step 04

Spend the progress on one property.

Multiply progress by the distance the thing has to travel and write it to transform. Horizontal galleries are translateX(−p × overflow). Scrubbed reveals are scaleY or a clip. Counters are just Math.round(p × n).

Transform and opacity are composited on the GPU. Everything else drags layout or paint into your frame — see step six.

Step 05

Never render the raw value.

Write the target to a variable, and each frame move the rendered value a fraction of the way toward it: value += (target − value) × 0.08. The render now trails the input by a few frames, and that lag is the entire difference between "a website" and "a site that feels made".

This is what smooth-scroll libraries sell. You can have it in one line — they add the rest of the scroll model around it.

Step 06

Stay inside 16.7 milliseconds.

At 60fps that's the whole budget for your maths, layout, paint and composite. Animate transform and opacity and you skip layout and paint entirely. Touch top, width or box-shadow on every frame and you won't make it — and on a mid-range phone you'll miss it by a lot.

Read layout once per frame, write after. Interleaving getBoundingClientRect() with style writes forces the browser to recompute, repeatedly, inside your loop.

The six steps, stacked

Every scroll-driven effect you've admired is these in order. If one of them is missing, you can usually tell which by how it feels.

01 / READ

Take the number

One value from the browser. Read it once per frame, never per event.

02 / MAP

Normalise to 0 → 1

Against a range you chose, clamped. Everything downstream reads this instead.

03 / PIN

Hold the stage

Sticky element, tall parent. The parent's height is your timeline.

04 / DRIVE

Progress × distance

Write it to transform. One property, one responsibility.

05 / EASE

Lag the render

Lerp toward the target. This is the step people feel and can't name.

06 / BUDGET

16.7ms, every frame

Composited properties only. Measure on the worst phone you support.
